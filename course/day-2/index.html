Pricing and estimating

About a 6-minute read, plus a short worksheet

Underpricing doesn't feel like a leak. It feels like winning bids. Today is about what an hour of work really costs you, and why the job you won might be the job that's costing you.

An hour costs more than the wage

When you price an hour of labor, it's tempting to start from what you pay the tech. But that hour also has to cover:

  • Payroll taxes and workers' comp on top of the wage
  • The truck: the payment, insurance, fuel, maintenance
  • Tools, uniforms, phones, software
  • Training, meetings, and all the time on the clock that nobody bills

That last one is the big one.

Paid hours vs. billable hours

A tech might be paid for 40 hours a week. But not all of those hours end up on an invoice. Drive time, parts runs, waiting on a customer, callbacks you don't charge for, the morning meeting: it all gets paid, and none of it gets billed.

So every hour you do bill has to carry the cost of the hours you don't.

Example (made-up numbers, not a benchmark)

A tech is paid for 2,080 hours a year and bills 1,300 of them. Every billed hour has to cover the cost of 1.6 paid hours.

If you set your rate as if every paid hour got billed, you're short on every single job, and you'd never see it on any one invoice.

The formula, once: true cost per billable hour = (yearly labor + yearly overhead) ÷ billable hours. The part people get wrong is the bottom of that fraction.

Pricing from the market

Most owners set prices by watching competitors. The problem is that you don't know if the other guy is making money either. If he's underpriced and you match him, you're both losing money and calling it competitive.

Your price has to start from your own costs. What the market will bear tells you how much room you have above that, not where to start.

Estimates that drift

Even a good rate leaks if the hours on the estimate don't match the hours on the job. The usual causes:

  • Drive time left off, or underestimated
  • A return trip for a part nobody planned for
  • "While you're here" extras that never made it onto the paperwork

You don't need to fix every estimate. You need to know which direction they drift and by how much.
