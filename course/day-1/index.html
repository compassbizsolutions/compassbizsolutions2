Run Your Shop Like a Business · Day 1 of 5

Know your real numbers

About a 5-minute read, plus a short worksheet

Most trades shops keep 2 to 3 cents of every dollar they bring in. Industry benchmarks say 20 cents is doable. On $1 million in revenue, that's the difference between $20,000 and $200,000 in your pocket. Today is about seeing which side of that gap you're on.

Revenue isn't profit

A full schedule feels like a healthy business. But you can be booked solid and still lose money, because revenue only tells you how much came in. It says nothing about how much stayed.

Two numbers tell you what stayed:

  • Gross margin is what's left after the job's own costs: the labor on that job, the materials, the fuel to get there, disposal.
  • Net profit is what's left after everything: insurance, trucks and their payments, the shop, software, phones, office help, and your own pay.
Example (made-up numbers)

A $1,000 job. Labor on the job $350, materials $250, fuel and disposal $50. That leaves $350 gross (35%).

Now each job also has to carry its share of overhead: insurance, trucks, the phone bill, software. Say that's $250 per job. That leaves $100 net (10%), and that's before you've paid yourself anything.

Pay yourself first, on paper

A lot of owners never put themselves on the payroll. Whatever's left at the end of the year is "profit," and that's what they live on.

Here's the problem: if you'd have to pay someone $70,000 to do your job, then the first $70,000 of "profit" is really just your wage. If your business made $60,000 and you took all of it home, the business didn't make money. You did, by working for less than your job is worth.

To see the real picture, subtract a fair wage for your own job before you call anything profit.

Where the money hides

The gap between gross and net is where profit leaks. In trades shops, it's almost always the same handful of places:

Rates that haven't movedEstimates that run overCallbacks and reworkHours nobody billsNo-shows and empty slotsDrive time and parts runsInvoices sent lateBalances nobody chasesMaterials sold at costExtras never invoicedQuotes never followed upToo few reviews, too few calls

Over the next four days we'll walk through the biggest ones: pricing, scheduling, getting paid, and making fixes stick.
