Getting paid (and your own time)

About 6 minutes, in short slides, plus a worksheet

You did the work. So why isn't the money here yet? Every day an invoice sits unsent or unpaid, you're lending your customer money for free. Today covers the gaps that slow your cash, and the math on the hours you spend chasing it.

The three cash gaps

  1. Job done → invoice sent. If invoices go out at the end of the week, or whenever you get to them, every job waits days before the clock even starts.
  2. Invoice sent → paid. No reminder means the invoice sits at the bottom of someone's pile.
  3. Balances nobody chases. Old invoices that everyone has quietly given up on.

Slow cash costs more than it looks. It forces you to float payroll and materials on a credit card, which adds interest on top of the money you're already waiting for.

Work you did but never billed

The most expensive leak is the one with no invoice at all: the extra part, the change the customer asked for on site, the second trip. It happened, it cost you, and nobody wrote it down.

Take the awkward out of collections

Most owners avoid chasing money because it feels personal. The way around that is a standard schedule that every invoice follows, so nobody's singled out. Same reminders, same days, every time. When it's routine, customers stop taking it personally, and so do you.

The math on your own time

Here's the part most owners skip. How many hours a week do you spend on invoices, follow-ups, scheduling and paperwork?

Example (plug in your own numbers)

8 hours a week on the office is about 35 hours a month. If an hour of your time on a job bills at $95, that's about $3,300 a month of work you could have billed instead.
