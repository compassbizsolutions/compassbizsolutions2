How to raise your prices without losing customers

The math that takes the fear out of a price increase, step by step, with a calculator and a short script you can use for January.

By Jen Voiselle · Compass Business Solutions

Here's the short answer on how to raise prices without losing customers: pick a number based on your real costs, tell your regulars plainly and early, and give new quotes the new price right away. Then do one bit of math, because it's what lets you sleep that night.

The math: a price increase lets you lose a surprising number of jobs before you make less money. In the example below, an 8% increase means you could lose about 1 in 5 jobs and still keep the same profit. Lose only a few and you're up about $10,000 a year.

Your numbers will be different. That's what the calculator further down is for.

Step 1: Know what you keep from each job

Before you change a price, know what's left after the job pays for itself: labor, materials and the truck. (Example numbers, rounded.)

Average job$400
Job costs: tech's time, parts, truck−$280
Left from each job (30%)$120
× jobs a year400
Left per year, to cover the office, you and profit$48,000

Step 2: Raise the price and watch the gap grow

Now raise prices 8%. The job costs don't change. Every extra dollar goes straight to the "left over" line.

New average job ($400 + 8%)$432
Job costs (same as before)−$280
Left from each job now$152

That's an 8% price change but a 27% jump in what you keep from each job ($120 to $152). That's why small price changes matter so much more than they feel like they should.

Step 3: How many customers can you lose and still come out ahead?

This is the number that matters. You used to need 400 jobs to clear $48,000. At $152 a job, you only need:

$48,000 ÷ $152 per job316 jobs
Jobs you could lose and still break even84 (21%)

The quick formula is increase ÷ (margin + increase). Here that's 8% ÷ (30% + 8%) = about 21%.

Now be realistic. Say a few price shoppers leave and you lose 5% of your jobs, 20 out of 400:

380 jobs × $152$57,760
Before the increase$48,000
You're ahead by$9,760 a year

And you did 20 fewer jobs to get there. Fewer evenings, less wear on the truck. Not a bad trade.

Step 4: How to tell your customers

The math gets you the courage. How you say it is how you keep the customers. A few rules that work for any trade:

  • New quotes get the new price now. Nobody new knows your old price.
  • Regulars get notice. About 30 days is fair. Honor anything already quoted or signed.
  • Keep it short. Two or three sentences. No apology, no essay, no discount in the same breath.
  • Say it once, the same way to everyone. Put it on the invoice, in an email or a text. Not a different story for each customer.

Something like this:

"Hi Mary, a quick heads up: starting January 1, our rates are going up a bit to keep up with the cost of parts, insurance and fuel. Anything already scheduled or quoted stays at today's price. Thanks for trusting us with your home. We don't take it lightly."

That's it. Most people have seen prices go up everywhere. What they remember is whether you showed up on time and did the job right.

What to do with your number

  • If you could lose 15% or more and still break even, you've got more room than you think. Fear is usually the only thing in the way.
  • If the margin per job is under 20%, fix the job costs first. A price increase on a leaky job just makes a bigger leak.
  • If you haven't raised prices in two years or more, you've already taken a pay cut. Your costs went up whether your prices did or not.
  • Pick a date and write it down. January 1 is easy for everyone to remember.

Questions owners ask

How much can I raise my prices before I lose money?

It depends on what you keep from each job. A simple rule: divide your price increase by (your margin per job plus the increase). If you keep 30% of each job and raise prices 8%, you could lose about 21% of your jobs and still make the same profit as before.

How much notice should I give customers before a price increase?

There's no single rule, but about 30 days is a common, fair window for regular customers. New quotes can use the new price right away. Honor quotes and agreements already signed until they end or renew.

Should I explain why I'm raising my prices?

Briefly, yes. One or two plain sentences about higher costs for parts, insurance or fuel is enough. Don't apologize, don't write a long letter, and don't offer a discount in the same breath.
