How to price a maintenance agreement

What one member really costs you, what to charge, and how to check you're not paying customers to be on your plan. Step by step, with a calculator.

By Jen Voiselle · Compass Business Solutions

Here's the short answer on how to price a maintenance agreement: add up everything one member costs you for a year (the visits, the drive time, the supplies, the discount you promised and the office time), then set a price that covers that cost and still leaves you a real margin.

In the example below, one member costs about $234 a year to take care of. To keep 30% of what they pay, the plan comes to about $334 a year, or $28 a month. A lot of owners guess $149 or $199 because that's what the shop down the road charges. Then every tune-up quietly costs them money.

It works the same for a furnace tune-up, a water heater flush, a pool opening and closing, a spring and fall cleanup, or a gutter and roof check. Your numbers will be different. That's what the calculator is for.

Step 2: Add the perks and the paperwork

Perks feel free when you write them on the brochure. They aren't. A 15% repair discount is real money every time a member calls you.

2 visits a year × $87$174
15% off repairs, on a member who spends $300 a year with you$45
Office time: booking, reminders, billing, renewals$15
Real cost of one member, per year$234

That office line is the one that gets skipped. Someone has to call Mrs. Thompson three times to get the fall visit on the calendar. That time is part of the plan.

Step 3: Set the price with margin, not markup

Here's where the math trips people up. Adding 30% to your cost does not leave you 30%.

Cost + 30% markup ($234 × 1.30)$304
What you actually keep ($70 ÷ $304)23%

To keep 30%, divide by what's left after your margin:

$234 ÷ (1 − 0.30)$334 a year
Or monthly ($334 ÷ 12)about $28
You keep, per member, per year$100

Same trap as pricing parts. The materials markup guide has the markup vs. margin math in more detail.

Step 4: Check it against what they'd pay without the plan

A plan has to be a good deal for the customer too, or nobody signs up. Say your regular price for one of these visits is $189.

2 visits at the regular price$378
The plan$334
Member saves, before the repair discount$44

Plus 15% off repairs and they're first on the list when the busy season hits. That's an easy yes. And you still keep $100 a member. With 100 members, that's about $10,000 a year, booked into your slow weeks, before you count a single repair they call you for.

If the plan comes out more than paying per visit, don't slash the price. Trim a perk, or look at why the visit costs so much to deliver. Drive time between members is usually the culprit, so group them by town.

What to do with your number

  • If your current plan price is below your cost line, every renewal is a small loss. Raise it at renewal time, with notice, and honor what's already signed.
  • If the member savings come out negative, trim a perk or tighten your routes before you touch the price.
  • If the office line made you wince, automate the reminders and renewals. That's a process fix, not a pricing fix.
  • Put the terms in writing: what's included, how it renews, how to cancel. Clear terms mean fewer awkward calls.
  • Book the visits for your slow season. A plan's real gift is steady work in the weeks the phone goes quiet.

Questions owners ask

How much should a maintenance agreement cost?

Add up what one member really costs you for a year: the visits (tech time, drive time and supplies), the cost of any repair discount, and office time. Then divide by (1 minus the margin you want to keep). In one example, a member who costs $234 a year to serve, at a 30% margin, comes to about $334 a year, or about $28 a month.

Should I charge for a maintenance plan monthly or yearly?

Either can work. Monthly is easier for customers to say yes to and keeps the plan renewing on its own; yearly puts cash in the bank up front. If you offer monthly, remember card processing fees come out of every payment, so price them in.

What should a maintenance agreement include?

Keep it simple: the scheduled visits, priority scheduling, and one clear perk like a repair discount or no trip charge. Every perk has a cost, so only offer what you've put a number on. Put the terms in writing: what's included, how it renews and how to cancel.
