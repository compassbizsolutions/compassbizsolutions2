What should your service call fee be?

The simple math behind a service call fee that pays for the visit, step by step, with a calculator so you can run it with your own numbers.

By Jen Voiselle · Compass Business Solutions

Here's the short answer: your service call fee should at least cover what it costs you to get a tech to the door, find the problem, and handle the call in the office. Then it needs a little on top for overhead and profit. In the example below, that's about $70 just to break even, and about $100 once a fair margin is added.

If you're charging $49, or "free estimates," every no-buy visit is coming out of your pocket. Not because you're bad at business. Because nobody ever sat down and did the math on a visit. So let's do it.

Step 1: Know what a paid hour really costs

Drive time and diagnosis time are paid hours. So the first number you need is your tech's real cost per paid hour: wage, payroll taxes, workers' comp, benefits, truck and tools.

I walked through that math in What does your tech really cost per hour? In that example, a $30/hr tech really costs about $47.48 for every hour on the clock. We'll use that here. (Example number. Yours will be different.)

Step 2: Add up the cost of one visit

Before anyone fixes anything, a service call uses three chunks of time. (Example numbers.)

Drive there and back, 50 minutes at $47.48/hr$39.57
Look, test, find the problem, 30 minutes at $47.48/hr$23.74
Office: book it, confirm it, invoice it, 15 minutes at $28/hr$7.00
What one visit costs you$70.31

That's your break-even service call fee. Charge less than that and you lose money every time the customer says "thanks, I'll think about it."

Step 3: Add your margin

Break-even keeps the lights on for the visit, but it doesn't pay for the office, insurance, software, or you. So add a margin.

The easy way: divide your cost by (1 minus the margin you want). For a 30% margin:

Cost of one visit$70.31
÷ (1 − 30% margin)0.70
Service call fee$100.44

Why divide instead of adding 30%? Adding 30% to $70.31 gives $91.40. But the $21.09 you keep is only about 23% of that price. Dividing gets you a true 30% margin. (Same trap as in the materials markup guide.)

In this example, a service call fee around $100 covers the visit and leaves room for the business. Round to a number you're comfortable saying out loud on the phone, but round up, not down.

Step 4: What "free estimates" really cost

Now say you waive the fee on 8 visits a week, for 50 weeks a year:

What to do with your number

  • If your current fee is under break-even, raise it for new customers first. Existing customers get a heads-up before it changes.
  • Say it on the phone, every time. "Our service call is $100. That covers the trip and finding the problem." Then put the same line in the confirmation text. Surprises are what start arguments, not the fee.
  • Decide on credits on purpose. If you credit the fee toward the repair, make sure your repair prices already cover the trip. Otherwise every booked job is a free visit in disguise.
  • If drive time is the big line, look at zones or routing before you look at the fee. A Cape run in summer traffic is not the same visit as one across town.

Questions owners ask

How do you calculate a service call fee?
Add up what one visit costs you before any repair starts: round-trip drive time and diagnosis time at your tech's real cost per paid hour, plus the office time to book, confirm and invoice the call. That's your break-even. Divide it by (1 minus the margin you want) to get a fee that also helps cover overhead and profit.

Should I credit the service call fee toward the repair?
It's a choice, not a rule. Crediting the fee when the customer approves the work can help close more jobs, but only if your repair prices already include the cost of getting there. If they don't, the credit quietly turns every booked job back into a free visit.

What's the difference between a trip charge and a diagnostic fee?
A trip charge usually covers getting a tech and truck to the door. A diagnostic fee covers the time spent finding the problem. Many shops roll both into one service call fee. Whatever you call it, say the amount and what it covers when the customer books, so nobody is surprised.
